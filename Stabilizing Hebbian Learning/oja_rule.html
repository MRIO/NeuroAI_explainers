NeuroAI explainers · Oja's rule

Keeping weights under control

Hebb's postulate asks a synapse to strengthen whenever its presynaptic neuron is active while the postsynaptic neuron fires. Taken literally the rule has no brake, since a stronger synapse drives the output harder, which strengthens the synapse again. Erkki Oja added a decay term that each synapse can compute from what it already sees, and with it the weights stay bounded and come to encode the strongest correlated pattern in the input.

1 · One update, step by step

Holding Hebb's horses

Let's study how a weight changes under the Hebbian rule, and what happens once a term is added that counters its boundless growth. Take an input neuron i connected to an output neuron j through a weight wij, with the output given by the weighted sum of all inputs, aj = Σi wij ai. The Hebbian change of the weight is the product ai aj, and averaged over many input patterns this product is the correlation between the two activities. Since aj is itself driven through wij, every increase of the weight raises the correlation that caused it, so under the Hebbian rule alone the weight grows without bound.

The counter term has to leave the synapses free to grow, while stopping them from growing past a bound. Oja's choice is aj² wij:

Δwij = η ( ai aj − aj² wij )

Three properties make it a good choice. It is proportional to the weight, so a small synapse loses almost nothing and keeps growing, while a large one loses a lot. It is gated by the output, so a synapse decays only when neuron j is active, which is also the only time the Hebbian term can make it grow. And it carries one more power of aj than the Hebbian term: doubling every weight doubles the Hebbian term and multiplies the decay by eight, so as the weights grow the decay first catches up with the growth and then overtakes it. Everything it needs, the weight, the output and the input, is available at the synapse itself.

Taking aj out of the bracket shows where the bound lies for each synapse:

Δwij = η aj ( ai − aj wij )

A synapse grows while aj wij is smaller than its input ai and shrinks once it passes that point. Summed over all synapses onto j, this balance holds the total squared weight at one, as the figure below shows.

Multiplying each change by its weight and summing over the synapses onto j shows why the total settles at one:

Σi wij Δwij = η aj² ( 1 − Σi wij² )

Below one the Hebbian term wins and the weights grow, above one the decay wins and they shrink. Since the decay of each synapse is proportional to its own weight, it rescales all weights onto j by the same factor, 1 − ηaj², and the shape of the weight pattern is left to the Hebbian term.

The stronger of the two correlated groups takes over the neuron.

Now lower the probability of group A while the network runs. What decides the winner is how often a group is active multiplied by the square of its activity, which is what the correlations ⟨ai ak⟩ measure. Around 0.3 the weights are shared between the two groups, and below about 0.25 group B takes over even though its inputs are weaker.

2 · The same rule as an arrow

Two inputs, one direction

With two inputs, the two weights onto j, (w1j, w2j), can be drawn as an arrow in the plane of input activities, and every input pattern becomes a dot in that same plane. The output aj is the projection of a dot onto the arrow, so it is large for patterns that lie along the arrow and small for patterns that lie across it.

Each Hebbian step pulls the arrow toward the pattern just presented, in proportion to aj, and the patterns along the long axis of the cloud pull hardest; meanwhile the decay term holds the arrow at unit length. Together they rotate the weight vector until it lies along the first principal component of the inputs, the direction in which the inputs vary most, drawn dashed. At that point the neuron's output carries as much of the input variance as a single projection can.

Press play and watch the angle to PC1 fall toward zero. Then drag the diamond to rotate the cloud, or drag the tip of the arrow anywhere, and follow the weights as they realign; the slider makes the cloud rounder or flatter.

The rule, per synapse Δwij = η ( ai aj − aj² wij )

Here wij is the synapse from input neuron i to output neuron j, ai the presynaptic activity, aj = Σi wij ai the output of the linear neuron, and η a small learning rate. For small η and weights with Σi wij² = 1, the rule agrees to first order in η with a Hebbian step followed by rescaling the weights onto j to unit length.

E. Oja (1982). Simplified neuron model as a principal component analyzer. Journal of Mathematical Biology 15, 267–273.
