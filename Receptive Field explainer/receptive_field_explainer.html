Your visual cortex contains neurons that fire selectively — some for edges tilted left, others for tilted right, others for horizontal bars. This selectivity, called a receptive field, emerges from something surprisingly simple: weighted sums and a threshold. This explainer builds that intuition from scratch.

Part I

The McCulloch-Pitts Neuron

In 1943, Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts proposed the first mathematical model of a neuron. Their key insight: a neuron is a threshold device. It collects signals from many sources, combines them, and fires only if the combined signal crosses a minimum level.

Step 1 — Weighted inputs

Each incoming signal \(x_i\) is multiplied by a weight \(w_i\). The weight encodes the strength of the synaptic connection. Positive weights are excitatory; negative weights are inhibitory.

\[\text{weighted sum} = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + \cdots + w_n x_n = \mathbf{w}^{\top} \mathbf{x}\]

This is a dot product — or equivalently, a matrix multiplication of a row vector by a column vector. The result is a single number: the neuron's total input current.

Step 2 — The Heaviside threshold

The neuron fires (outputs 1) if the weighted sum exceeds a threshold \(\theta\), and stays silent (outputs 0) otherwise. The function that does this is the Heaviside step function, named after the self-taught mathematician Oliver Heaviside.

\[H(z) = \begin{cases} 1 & z \geq 0 \\ 0 & z < 0 \end{cases} \qquad \text{where } z = \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} - \theta\]

The neuron, complete

Putting it together, a McCulloch-Pitts neuron is entirely described by three things: its weight vector \(\mathbf{w}\), its inputs \(\mathbf{x}\), and its threshold \(\theta\).

function neuron(x, w, θ): z ← dot(w, x) − θ // linear combination minus threshold return H(z) // Heaviside: 1 if z ≥ 0, else 0

With weights \(w_1 = w_2 = 1\) and threshold \(\theta = 1.5\), the neuron fires only when both inputs are active — an AND gate, or coincidence detector. Both signals must arrive together to push the weighted sum past the threshold. This is one of the simplest forms of pattern recognition: detecting the co-occurrence of two events.

Part II

Receptive Fields & Pattern Recognition

What is a receptive field?

A neuron's receptive field is the region of sensory space — and the set of stimuli within it — that causes it to fire. In vision, this means a tiny patch of retina: a few photoreceptors whose signals converge onto one neuron.

A 16×16 retinal patch — each square is one photoreceptor (filled = illuminated). The outlined region is the neuron's 3×3 receptive field, positioned over a vertical edge of the house wall.

Topographic projections

Crucially, retinal projections to the cortex are largely topographic — nearby cells on the retina connect to nearby cells in the cortex. This spatial order is preserved across multiple processing stages. A 3×3 patch of retina maps, roughly in order, to 9 input connections on a cortical neuron.

Concept — Topographic mapping

Hover over, or tap, a retinal pixel to trace its projection. Spatial order is approximately preserved.

From 2D grid to 1D vector

Topography tells us which pixel connects to which weight. A neuron, however, never sees a grid; it receives a list of currents arriving along its dendrites, so both the stimulus and the weight matrix must be flattened into nine-element vectors, read row by row, before the dot product can be taken.

This flattening is a bookkeeping convention with no biological counterpart, and what matters is only that the correspondence is preserved, so that pixel \((r, c)\) always lands at the same position in the vector, and therefore on the same weight.

Each 3 × 3 matrix becomes a 9 × 1 column vector, read row by row. The transpose keeps the printed form compact; both results are columns.

Both vectors have the same length, nine, so their dot product is well defined; the weight at position \(i\) multiplies the pixel at position \(i\), and the spatial correspondence that topography established is now carried entirely by the ordering of the vector.

// Flattening: 3×3 matrix → 9×1 vector (row-major order) flatten(M): v ← [] for r in 0..2: for c in 0..2: v.append( M[r][c] ) // index i = 3·r + c return v // length 9 // Then the neuron is simply: y ← H( dot(w_flat, x_flat) − θ )

The 3×3 receptive field

We model a neuron that receives from exactly 9 photoreceptors arranged in a 3×3 grid. Each pixel sends one input \(x_i \in \{0, 1\}\). The neuron has 9 corresponding weights \(w_i\). Because both are flattened in the same order, their dot product correctly computes the weighted sum over the spatial patch.

The key question: what weights make a neuron prefer a specific pattern?

Think about it geometrically: the weights that best distinguish a pattern are those that look like the pattern. High weight where the pattern is bright; low or negative weight where it is dark. The dot product is then maximised for the preferred stimulus.

A network of detectors

Now suppose you want four neurons, each preferring a different orientation. Each neuron has its own weight vector but shares the same 9 inputs. The whole layer is described by a weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}\), where each row is one neuron's weights:

\[\mathbf{y} = H\!\left(\mathbf{W}\,\mathbf{x} - \theta\right)\]
// Feed-forward network of 4 orientation detectors function FFNN(W, x, θ): for each row w_i in W: y_i ← H( dot(w_i, x) − θ ) return [y_1, y_2, y_3, y_4]

Notice that the four rows of \(\mathbf{W}\) describe four different neurons reading the same nine inputs, which amounts to a fully connected layer over a single patch. A convolution inverts that arrangement, applying one weight vector at every position of a larger image, with the same nine numbers reused throughout, so that a single detector reports wherever its preferred feature happens to occur. That reuse, known as weight sharing, is what turns the neuron you have just tuned into a convolutional filter, and layers of such filters stacked on one another are where deep convolutional networks begin. Working out what your vertical-bar detector would report as it passed across the house above, and why the roof edges would leave it silent, is a good way into that literature.

Part III

Assumptions & Their Limits

Every model rests on simplifications. These are not flaws — they are design choices that make the model tractable. Understanding them is what separates a user of a model from a scientist who can extend it.

  • A1
    Continuous, static inputs. We used pixel values of exactly 0 or 1 — the stimulus doesn't change over time. Real photoreceptors produce graded, noisy, time-varying signals. Ask yourself: under what illumination conditions might binary inputs be a reasonable approximation?
  • A2
    Integration in a single time step. Our neuron collects all 9 inputs simultaneously and fires or not, once. Biological neurons integrate inputs over tens of milliseconds, and the timing between inputs matters. Look up coincidence detection and spike-timing-dependent plasticity for when this assumption breaks down — and why it might matter.
  • A3
    The Heaviside (all-or-nothing) output. We output exactly 0 or 1. Many real neurons fire at graded rates and are better modeled by a sigmoid or ReLU activation. Yet in the primary visual cortex, many cells do behave approximately as threshold units for their preferred stimuli.
  • A4
    Strict topography. We assumed the spatial layout of the retina is perfectly preserved in the weight matrix — pixel (0,0) connects to weight (0,0). The cortex is largely topographic, but with significant scatter, magnification factors, and remapping. Research: what happens to receptive field structure at the edges of V1?
  • A5
    Linear combination. Synaptic currents sum linearly in our model. Dendrites, however, can perform non-linear operations — local dendritic spikes, NMDA receptor dynamics. Under what conditions can a real dendrite be approximated as a simple summing device?

Nevertheless — this model works.

Despite every simplification, the McCulloch-Pitts neuron predicts the existence of orientation-selective cells in V1, explains why their preferred stimuli resemble their weight vectors, and is the computational unit underlying modern convolutional neural networks. Hubel and Wiesel discovered orientation selectivity in 1959 — the model anticipated it. That is the hallmark of a good scientific model: it is simple enough to understand, yet powerful enough to surprise you with what it predicts.

Research directions: Explore the conditions under which each assumption holds in the cortex. Look up integrate-and-fire neurons, rate-coded models, and sparse coding. You now have the foundation.
