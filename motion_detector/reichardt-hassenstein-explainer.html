Explorable · Insect Vision

How does a fly, with no cortex, know which way something is moving?

A single photoreceptor can only report how bright things are, right now, at one point in space. It has no way to encode motion by itself. Yet flies dodge swatting hands in milliseconds. This is the story of the circuit that makes that possible — built from nothing more than two sensors, a delay, and a multiplication.

00 — DefinitionWhat is motion perception?

Motion perception is the inference of velocity — how a feature's position changes over time — from a sequence of instantaneous, static snapshots of the world. It is not a raw input; it has to be computed.

A single sensor sampling light intensity at one location can tell you that its input got brighter or dimmer. It cannot, on its own, tell you that the brightening is part of something moving in a direction. Direction and speed only become available once you compare what one sensor sees with what a different, nearby sensor saw a moment before. That comparison — space plus a time delay — is the entire idea behind everything below.

01 — One pipelineWorld → retina → detector

What follows is one continuous chain, not three separate ideas. A dot moves through physical space. That motion projects onto the fly's one-dimensional retina as a sweep through visual angle — the angle φ between the fly's forward axis and the line of sight to the dot. Two points on that retina feed straight into a Reichardt–Hassenstein detector, whose two input branches are the same two receptors highlighted below. The correlator itself has no idea what "50 cm/s" means. It only knows whether a remembered signal and a live signal happened to coincide. The exact same peak response could come from a fast dot with widely spaced sensors, or a slow dot with closely spaced sensors — τ = Δt either way. The unit only acquires a meaning in physical units — ωpref = Δφ / τ — once you also know how far apart its two sensors are.

02 — TilingOne pair becomes an array

A single detector reports on one location. Real motion-vision systems tile the whole field with a repeating array of identical detectors — a row of receptors on top, and between every adjacent pair, one delay-and-multiply unit. Neighboring detectors share receptors, so the field is covered without gaps. This is the columnar organization seen throughout insect optic lobes.

Now generalize once more: give every point in the field neighbors in every direction, not just left–right. That's the step from this one-dimensional array to a real, two-dimensional eye.

03 — SynthesisA 2D field of eight directional detectors

The connectivity rule is simple and completely local: for every pair of cells that sit at a fixed distance and a fixed relative position in the image, wire up one detector. Take a 50×50 patch of visual field. For each pixel, pair it with the neighbor one step away in each of the 8 compass directions (N, NE, E, SE, S, SW, W, NW), and drop a delay-and-multiply unit on that pair. The relative position of the two cells is the direction that detector prefers — a pixel paired with its eastern neighbor prefers rightward motion, a pixel paired with its northern neighbor prefers upward motion.

Collecting all the "paired-with-my-eastern-neighbor" units across the whole patch gives one full map of rightward motion; all the "paired-with-my-northern-neighbor" units give a separate map of upward motion, and so on. Eight relative positions, eight maps. (Pairing cells two or three steps apart instead of one would build detectors tuned to faster motion — same rule, larger Δ.)

04 — GeneralityThe same wiring, other senses

This circuit — a coincidence detector fed by one live line and one delay line — is not particular to compound eyes. Swap "photoreceptor" for "ear" and you get the Jeffress model of sound localization: neurons in the auditory brainstem receive input from both ears over axons that act as fixed delay lines, and each neuron fires maximally when a sound's interaural time difference happens to exactly cancel its own built-in delay. A population of these neurons, each tuned to a different delay, encodes the direction a sound came from — the same way a population of Reichardt detectors, each tuned to a different Δφ and τ, encodes the direction something moved.

Nothing in the wiring requires light, either. Two mechanoreceptors a few millimeters apart on the skin, feeding the same delay-and-multiply circuit, could in principle report the direction and speed of a stimulus brushing across the surface — an insect walking up your arm, or a raised dot moving under a fingertip. Any two-point sensor array with a fixed internal delay can become a velocity detector. What changes across sensory systems is the receptor; the algorithm stays the same.

Built from one idea, repeated: compare a delayed signal against a live one, at two points in space. One pair becomes a detector. A row of pairs becomes a 1D motion map. A grid of pairs, in eight directions, becomes optic flow.
