First return maps of a random recurrent network

A network of N sigmoidal neurons with Gaussian random couplings, iterated in discrete time. Plotting the mean activity at one step against the next turns its attractor into a picture, and a static random stimulus changes that picture.

Things to try

  • Slide σI from 0 up to 1.5. The stimulus pattern stays the same and only its amplitude grows. Where does the forced attractor become a torus, a cycle, a point?
  • Set σI to 0 and lower g below 5. Compare what you see with the mean-field line further down.
  • Open Deep in chaos and press New stimulus a dozen times, counting how often the forced dynamics stays chaotic. The paper's Table 2 shows how much individual networks scatter.
  • Open Period-60 cycle and raise g in steps of 0.01 towards 5.45. Where does the cycle give way to chaos?
  • Switch the observable to one neuron and pick one with a large positive or negative Ik (hover the stimulus strip below to find one).

Section 3.2 of the paper

Where the stimulus pushes each neuron

A neuron is silent when xj(t) < 0.01 and saturated when xj(t) > 0.99 for every recorded step; all others are dynamical. The paper attributes the reduction of dynamics to the shrinking dynamical population: neurons with a large |Ij| freeze, and the saturated ones then act on the rest as an extra threshold through the weights.

Fig. 2 and Table 2 of the paper

The stimulus as a step across the mean-field map

For N → ∞ the mean-field theory draws a sharp line between a stable fixed point and chaos (the paper's Fig. 2). Because the model is unchanged under (g, J, σθ) → (gJ, 1, σθ/J), the axes here are gJ and spread over J.

The stimulus enters the local field exactly like a second threshold with the opposite sign. With θ = I = 0 the forced network therefore behaves as an autonomous one whose threshold spread is √(σθ2 + σI2). Presenting the stimulus moves the network to the right on this map, towards the stable side (Sec. 3.1).

In finite networks the change is spread over a band. The shaded band runs from the mean gain at which the fixed point loses stability to the mean gain at which chaos appears, measured on 50 networks of 200 neurons (Table 2). Individual networks scatter widely around both.

Model and code

What runs when a slider moves

xj(t+1) = f( g · uj(t) ), f(y) = (1 + tanh y) / 2
uj(t) = Σi wij xi(t) − θj + Ij
mnet(t) = (1/N) Σj xj(t)

Weights follow the explainer convention: wij is the synapse from neuron i onto neuron j, so the heatmap's rows are senders and its columns receivers. The paper writes Jij for the weight from j to i, which makes its matrix the transpose of this one.

Under paper scaling a weight has mean J/N and variance J2/N, which keeps the summed input to a neuron finite as N grows (Sec. 2.1). The transfer function is the one printed with Eq. 1; its slope 2x(1 − x) matches the Jacobian 2g·diag(x(1 − x))·J of Sec. 2.2.

The autonomous run starts from a uniform random x(0), discards the transient and records the map. The forced run starts from the last autonomous state, discards the same transient and records again. The Lyapunov estimate follows a second trajectory 10−8 away and renormalises it every step; the label on each chip combines it with a check for exact periodicity, so read it as a rough diagnosis.
